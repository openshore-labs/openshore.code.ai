Competitive Landscape · Evidence Brief

Every capable coding agent runs on someone else’s servers. OpenShore runs on machines you own, and follows you from desk to pocket.

Here is the bet. The best coding agents are being rented, not owned. Your code, your context, and your workflow live on a vendor’s cloud, on their pricing, their model choices, their uptime, their data policy. OpenShore makes a different trade: it is a full coding agent that runs on your own hardware, private by construction, and it travels across your devices, a pocket model on the phone and your big models on the desk, synced over a network you own. That corner was empty until 2026. It is not any longer: a small, mostly open-source wave has started racing into it, which is a signal the demand is real and a warning that the moat is execution, not category. If the bet holds, the developers who care about owning their tools, and the ones who legally cannot send code to a cloud, will choose sovereignty without giving up the agent, and pick the most finished version of it. That is a claim we have to measure, not assert.

The lead argument · sovereignty and cross-device

Two moves rivals cannot copy without becoming a different company.

Rivals can match OpenShore on model quality by renting the best cloud model of the month. They cannot match it on where the work runs without unwinding the business they built. A cloud-agent company sells inference by the token; running fully on your hardware deletes its meter. And almost none of them travel: the coding agent lives in one place, a desktop IDE, a terminal, or a web tab, while the phone gets nothing or a thin chat. OpenShore does both at once. The reasoning model that quarterbacks the work runs on a box you own, specialists plug in by task, and the same stack answers from your iPhone over your own private network, or from a pocket model fully on the phone when there is no signal at all. Three separate bodies of evidence say this combination matters more than a feature list makes it look.

  1. A large share of developers legally or contractually cannot send code to a cloud model. Confidential source, regulated industries, air-gapped environments, and client contracts all forbid it. When a well-known firm banned public AI tools after engineers pasted proprietary code into ChatGPT, it named a constraint that a local-only agent removes by construction.

    Samsung internal ban on public generative AI, 2023 (Bloomberg). Cisco 2024 Data Privacy Benchmark: a majority of organizations restricted or limited generative-AI use over data and IP concerns. Transfer risk: Moderate. The constraint is real and documented; how many developers switch tools because of it is the open question.

  2. Local models have crossed the “good enough for real coding” line for a growing share of tasks. Open-weight coding models now handle a large fraction of everyday edits, refactors, and questions on consumer hardware, which is the enabling condition that makes a local-first agent viable rather than a compromise. The frontier still leads on the hardest tasks, which is exactly why OpenShore keeps cloud one deliberate tap away on your own key.

    Open coding-model benchmark trends (HumanEval, SWE-bench families), 2024–2026. Transfer risk: High. A fast-moving target. The direction is clear, but any specific “local matches cloud” claim dates quickly and must be re-checked, not asserted.

  3. People prefer a fixed price over a meter, even against their own arithmetic. The flat-rate bias is well documented: consumers choose flat tariffs over metered ones to avoid the anxiety of a running counter, even when metered would cost them less. A coding agent priced by the token keeps that counter running while you work; an owned agent removes it.

    Lambrecht & Skiera (2006), tariff-choice biases, J. Marketing Research. Transfer risk: Moderate. Robust in telecom and utilities; its pull on developer tool choice is an analogy to test.

Benchmarked What we know

Real constraints forbid cloud code in large segments. Local models are increasingly capable on consumer hardware. Buyers dislike meters and lock-in. Configured, self-built tools accrue attachment and switching cost. All established outside OpenShore.

Assumed What we don’t yet know

Whether sovereignty plus cross-device is a strong enough draw to move developers off a polished cloud agent, and how large the segment that will pay for it really is. The forces are real. The first-party evidence is zero.

The N=1 caveat. The founder is close to the ideal user: a builder who wants to own the stack and distrusts renting the tools. That makes OpenShore obviously valuable to the founder and tells us almost nothing about strangers. The sovereignty thesis is a claim about how many people share that preference strongly enough to switch and to pay. Only data from strangers can confirm it.

Why owning your stack compounds

Five forces explain why an owned, private, cross-device agent holds a user.

Ownership here is not a slogan. It is the condition that switches these forces on. A rented cloud agent, however polished, sits on the weak end of the first four, and one of the five is a caution, not a tailwind.

The IKEA effect, on your stack

Self-invested labor raises how much you value and keep the result. You pick the quarterback, place specialists by task, and build a crew of named agents. That configured stack is yours in a way a default never is.

Norton, Mochon & Ariely (2012), J. Consumer Psychology 22(3), 453-460.

Predicts for OpenShore: a stack the user assembled is valued above a faceless cloud default, which supports attachment and retention. The boundary condition holds: the effect only lands if the setup actually succeeds, so first-run must finish.

Benchmarked Transfer to a dev tool: Assumed

Loss aversion & the rug-pull

People weigh losses far more than equal gains. A rented agent can change its price, deprecate the model you rely on, alter its terms, or go down, and you absorb it. An owned, local stack cannot be taken away or repriced from the outside.

Kahneman & Tversky, prospect theory; endowment effect (Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, 1990).

Predicts for OpenShore: as cloud tools reprice, deprecate models, and shift terms, the value of an agent that cannot be changed out from under you rises. In 2025 to 2026 alone, Cursor moved to a usage meter and Windsurf was acquired and folded into Devin. Immunity to the rug-pull is a durable, structural draw.

Data sovereignty, the whole thesis in one line

Benchmarked constraint

For a large class of work, sending code to a third-party cloud is not a preference, it is prohibited. Confidential IP, regulated data, client contracts, and air-gapped environments all draw a hard line the mainstream tools cannot cross.

Cisco 2024 Data Privacy Benchmark (organizational limits on generative AI). Samsung public-AI ban, 2023 (Bloomberg). GDPR and data-residency regimes.

This is OpenShore’s design thesis, not a marketing angle. The others treat privacy as a setting. OpenShore makes it structural: the work runs where you put it, keys never leave your devices, everything is encrypted at rest, and cloud sees only the calls you deliberately make on your own account. For the segment under a hard line, that is not a nicer option, it is the only option that also gives them a real agent.

Flat price beats the meter

Buyers choose fixed prices over metered ones to escape the running counter, even when the meter would be cheaper. Token-metered agents keep that counter visible while you work; an owned agent removes it.

Lambrecht & Skiera (2006), tariff-choice biases, J. Marketing Research 43(2).

Predicts for OpenShore: a flat, owned price (free to chat, a small flat fee to build) reads as calmer than a meter, and removes the self-throttling that suppresses how much people use metered tools. It should raise depth of use.

Benchmarked Dev-tool transfer: Assumed

The privacy paradox

People say they value privacy, then routinely fail to pay for it or change behavior to protect it. Stated preference and revealed preference diverge sharply.

Norberg, Horne & Horne (2007), J. Consumer Affairs 41(1). Replicated widely.

Honesty flag: this is the mechanism that can break the thesis. If privacy converts only as a stated preference and not a purchase, the sovereignty draw collapses to a niche. The bet must rest on the segment with a hard constraint or a real cost, confidential-code, regulated, offline, cost-sensitive, not on privacy sentiment in the general developer market.

The landscape

Two axes, and a race is now forming for the top-right.

Two things separate every product here, and they are exactly the two halves of OpenShore’s bet. Sovereignty folds together three questions that move as one: does it run on your hardware or their cloud, is it open and yours or a locked box, and is it private or surveilled and metered. Reach is whether the agent lives on a single surface or truly travels across your devices, pocket and desk, synced over a network you own. Plot both, and the top-right was empty until 2026, when a small, mostly open-source wave (the plum pins) began racing into it. The horizontal axis is sovereignty, and it is the switching-cost and trust axis. Ownership, openness, and privacy move together: the products on the right run on your hardware, keep your keys, and cannot reprice or deprecate the tool from the outside. The whole left column, the cloud agents, competes on model polish and re-earns your trust and your bill every month.
The vertical axis is reach. Almost every sovereign product is stranded on one surface, a desktop runner, an IDE plugin, or a phone-only app. Almost every cross-device product is a cloud that you do not own. Apple Intelligence is the telling near-miss: genuinely cross-device and private, but closed, not your models, and not a coding agent.
The top-right is where both are true, and until 2026 it was empty. Now OpenMono and Paseo are racing into it. OpenShore’s bet is not being alone there, it is arriving as the finished, local-models-first product while the newcomers are terminal tools, cloud-agent remotes, or general assistants. The corner is contested · read this honestly

An empty quadrant is partly an artifact of the axes you choose, and this one is filling in real time. The honest read is not “nobody is here,” it is “a nascent cohort is converging, and OpenShore has to out-execute it.” What the newcomers are, precisely:

  • OpenMono is the near-twin: a local-LLM coding agent (llama.cpp), home GPU plus iPhone control, open source and free, shipping on both app stores, with an explicit “AI shouldn’t have a meter” thesis. It is the single closest competitor and it is live.
  • Paseo (15k+ GitHub stars) nails cross-device and privacy, but it orchestrates other agents whose models are usually cloud. It is a sovereign remote control, not local-models-first.
  • OpenClaw and the phone-first tools are cousins, either general assistants or thin on the coding agent.

So where is OpenShore’s defensible edge? Not the category, which is now contested. It is product finish: a native, polished app rather than a terminal or a dev kit, real on-device phone inference rather than a remote for a box, a curated quarterback-plus-specialists stack, and a commercial product with launch-to-store, crew, and projects. The moat is execution and integration, and the newcomers validate the demand rather than refute it.

Discipline

What we can claim today, and what we must measure next.

The forces are real and the map positions are verifiable. The OpenShore-specific predictions stay hypotheses until strangers, especially the constrained segments, show up in the data.

Can claim now Benchmarked

  • OpenShore is in a tiny, brand-new cluster (with OpenMono and Paseo) that is a full coding agent, local-first, and cross-device. It is the only one arriving as a finished, local-models-first native product across phone and desk, rather than a terminal tool or a cloud-agent remote.
  • The constraints are real: large segments legally cannot send code to a cloud, and cloud tools do reprice, deprecate, and go down.
  • Behavioral forces (self-built attachment, loss aversion, flat-rate preference) point toward retention and depth of use for an owned tool (direction only).

Must instrument Assumed, no OpenShore data

  • Segment pull: activation and paid conversion split by constrained (confidential-code, regulated, offline) vs. general developers. The number the whole thesis rests on.
  • Sovereignty willingness-to-pay: do people pay the flat fee to build, or stall at free chat? Tests the privacy-paradox risk head on.
  • Cross-device usage: what share actually pair a phone to a desk, and does pairing lift retention, or is it a demo that goes unused?
  • Local first-run success: share who reach a first working local model on their own hardware. The IKEA boundary condition, failed setup erases the effect.
  • Local-only sufficiency: how often users escalate to cloud. If it is constant, the local promise is thin; if rare, it is real.
  • Retention vs. stack investment: does building a crew and projects raise D30, testing the self-built switching cost directly?

The one cheap experiment that most reduces uncertainty

Do the constrained segments convert to paid at a higher rate than general developers?

Primary metric
Free-to-paid conversion, segmented by a one-question intake: is your code under a constraint that forbids cloud AI (confidential, regulated, air-gapped, contractual), yes or no.
Guardrails
Local first-run success rate (setup must finish), local-only sufficiency (escalation frequency), support-contact rate.
Design
Onboard the first stranger cohorts. Tag each by the constraint question at intake. Compare paid conversion and D30 across the two segments. Even a few hundred users gives a directional read on whether the constrained segment is the real market.
Decision rule
Set before running: if constrained-segment conversion clearly beats general, lead the product and the story at that segment. If both convert similarly and high, sovereignty is broader than assumed. If neither converts, the privacy-paradox risk was decisive and the wedge must be re-found.

The discipline. The founder is close to the ideal user, so the founder’s own conviction is not evidence, it is a sample of one who already believes. Every “Assumed” claim here becomes “Measured” only when strangers, and specifically the constrained segments, activate, pay, and retain in the data. Until then this is a grounded set of hypotheses, not a proven scorecard.

Evidence limits, stated plainly. The map positions and capability columns are verifiable and current as of this brief, but this is a fast-moving field: ownership, mobile plans, and model support shift, so re-check any single competitor claim before you act on it. The top-right is contested, not empty. An earlier draft of this map overstated OpenShore’s isolation; the honest picture is a nascent 2026 cohort (OpenMono, a near-twin shipping on both app stores; Paseo, 15k+ stars; OpenClaw and phone-first tools as cousins) racing into the same corner. That validates demand and means the moat is execution, not category. The behavioral forces are well established, but the privacy paradox is a genuine threat to the thesis, not a footnote: privacy as sentiment does not reliably convert to purchase, so the bet rests on segments with a hard constraint or a real cost. The “local models are good enough” claim dates the fastest of all. OpenShore has no funnel or retention data yet. Every OpenShore-specific prediction here is a graded hypothesis awaiting data from strangers, not a measured result.
